#### Scene 1 — A

Three of them. Twelve meters of corridor between the pallet stacks and the gantry stairs, one sodium lamp doing the work of four, and no cover in any direction that mattered. It was a killing shape. Everybody in it knew whose.

They were wrong about whose.

Whatever happens, happens.

The Jericho cleared leather while the man on the left was still deciding — the one with the toothpick, the one whose hands said he'd practiced a fast draw in a mirror for years, and it was fast, it was genuinely fast, it would have been enough against anyone who paused. Two rounds took him in the chest before the toothpick hit the ground.

The trick to a corridor is you never stop walking. Standing still is agreeing to the geometry.

The second man had his sash tied wrong — left-handed knot, three weeks in the colors, maybe four — and he did the thing new men do, which is look at his friend falling instead of at the gun. There's a chain fall on that gantry approach hanging at knee height; there always has been; the dockhands curse it every shift. The new man backed into it, and the half-second it stole from him was the whole rest of his life. One round. He sat down against the pallets like a man deciding to rest, and rested.

The third one was a kid.

The kid was screaming and firing at the same time, eyes half shut, muzzle climbing, rounds going into the lamp, the ceiling, the dark — and one of the wild ones wasn't wild. Something tugged at his coat, low on the left, the way a stranger tugs your sleeve in a crowd, and he noted it the way you note a stranger and kept walking, and put the kid down before the scream finished. The kid's pistol still had the safety half-fumbled. He'd died working on it.

Then the corridor was just a corridor. Smoke standing in the sodium light. Three men who'd been paid by the day.

He came out the far side into the harbor air and the cold hit the sweat on him and that's when his left side introduced itself — a deep, wrong warmth under the ribs, and his hand came away red, redder than it had any right to be, and his knees told him something honest that he declined to hear. Through-and-through, low. The kind of wound that gives you hours and lies about how many.

Somewhere above, a klaxon started. Then a second, farther down the pier, and a third, until the whole dock was one long ugly chord — every hatch light coming up amber, every camera turning its slow head. Six shots in a steel corridor. The docks are a drum. Everyone had heard the solo.

And berth twelve — the berth, the one this whole bleeding walk was for — sat empty.

Mooring cleats bare. Deck plates still ticking with heat where a transport had squatted until not long ago. An hour, maybe less. Somebody had moved the bounty while the docks were busy watching something else, which meant somebody had known to, which meant the three men behind him hadn't been guarding a prize. They'd been guarding a delay.

Blood was finding the waistband of his trousers, patient as rent.

From the stairwells back down the pier: boots. A lot of them, moving the way boots move when they're paid better than the last three were.

#### Scene 1 — B

The corridor is eleven meters of grated steel and two working lights, and you have counted both of these things before you finish drawing, because that is the whole plan: know the room better than the men standing in it.

Three of them. Red sashes. The one on the left has his tied wrong — knotted like a shoelace, like somebody's little brother playing dress-up. The middle one has done this before; you can tell because he's already moving sideways, off his partners' line. The right one has a wedding ring and a shotgun and the shotgun is still pointed at the floor, because nobody expects the man with no cover to come *forward*.

You come forward.

The Jericho speaks once and the middle one — the professional, the dangerous one, first because he's first — folds at the hip like a chair collapsing. You're already past where you were. That matters. In a corridor the geometry is the only friend you get, and you know this gantry: the chain hoist hanging dead center at head height, the drainage lip that catches boots, the way the second light throws your shadow *behind* you if you stay under it. The shotgun swings up and you put your shoulder into the hanging chain and it sails into the space you just left and takes the buckshot meant for your chest. Sparks. Chain-song. You fire under it, twice, and the wedding ring hits the grating and doesn't move.

The kid.

The kid has his eyes half shut and his pistol pushed out in front of him like he's warding something off, and he's firing, three, four, wild, and you shoot him through the sternum at four meters because he is between you and the far side and this was never going to be a story where that meant nothing.

Then it's quiet. That fast. The chain still swinging, ticking against a stanchion, keeping time for nobody.

You walk. Eleven meters becomes six becomes the door at the far end, and it's only when you put your hand on the frame that your side tells you about it — low, left, under the ribs, one of the kid's wild ones, and it doesn't hurt yet, it's just *warm*, warm and then wet, and your hand comes away from your shirt red in the bad light. Your body deciding to tell you the truth now that the truth can't get you killed. Considerate of it.

Behind you the dock is waking up. Klaxon first, then floods, big white beams starting their slow sweep down the pier — every ear on this dock heard the Jericho, and the Jericho has a voice people remember. Whatever quiet this job had, you just spent all of it in nine seconds.

And the ledger, since you're bleeding for it: the bounty's been gone an hour. Somebody moved him while the whole dock watched something shinier. Three men just died holding a door to an empty room, and the only one who might know where the room emptied *to* is the kid — because the kid isn't dead. You can hear him. Wet breathing against the wall, sash knotted like a shoelace, hand pressed flat over the hole in him like he can keep himself in.

The floods are maybe ninety seconds from this end of the pier. Your shirt is sticking to you. The kid's eyes find yours down the length of the corridor, and he doesn't look afraid of you, which is worse. He looks like he's waiting to find out what you are.

Ninety seconds. Maybe less.

#### Scene 2 — A

The bowl goes down first. Then you, into the chair across from her, and the steam comes up between you like something the galley rented for the occasion.

Third night of noodles. Nobody's said the number out loud since the payout cleared into somebody else's account — a man with faster paperwork and a slower conscience — but the number is at the table anyway. It sits where the beef would be.

Faye doesn't look up. Chopsticks moving, eyes on the broth like it owes her money too. She waits one beat, two, for the thing that usually comes with a second bowl — the crack about her appetite, the itemized bill delivered deadpan — and it doesn't come, and you watch her decide something about that.

You had a sentence. You'd been carrying it since the dock, turning it over in your pocket like a coin you weren't sure was real currency anymore. Not the words — you never had the words — but the shape of it, the weight. Set the bowl down, let it sit there a second, then say the thing. Simple. Except the bowl went down and the second came where the sentence was supposed to be, and now the silence is doing the talking, and silence is a translator who works for the other side.

Because here's what she hears, chopsticks slowing, shoulders coming up a half-inch the way they do: she hears the ledger closing. She hears *don't worry about it* in the way people say it to someone they've stopped expecting anything from. The bowl isn't an apology in the language she reads. It's severance. It's the last civil gesture before a door.

She eats it anyway. All of it, unhurried, because Faye Valentine does not leave food on a table she might not be invited back to.

Then she's up, rinsing the bowl in the little steel sink, her back to you, and her voice comes out with all the weight taken out of it, which is how you know exactly how much it weighs.

"Thanks for dinner." Water off. Bowl in the rack, precise, like she's leaving the place cleaner than she found it. "Next port with actual money in it, I'll get out of everyone's hair. Even things out."

She's in the hatchway now. One hand on the frame. Not gone yet — pausing the length of half a breath, the length of a sentence, if anyone at the table had one.

#### Scene 2 — B

The galley sink kept the crew's accounts: six empty cups stacked in a lean tower, chopsticks gone pale from rinsing. Three days since the bounty turned into somebody else's bounty. Nobody had said the number out loud since. The number said itself, every mealtime, in styrofoam.

You made two bowls. The sixty-woolong kind, from the back of the cabinet — the ones Jet had been saving for a day worth marking. This wasn't one. You took them anyway.

Faye had the table. Solitaire, the deck with the missing four of clubs, cigarette parked behind her ear — unlit, being rationed. She was cheating and losing anyway, red queen on red king, and she didn't look up when you crossed the room, and she didn't look up when you set the second bowl at her elbow and sat down across from her with nothing to say about the money.

You had a sentence, though. You'd built it in the corridor, tuned it against the hull-hum, cut it down from twenty words to nine to six. It cost about six hundred thousand woolongs, and it stayed where sentences like that live with you — behind the teeth, in the dark, keeping its own counsel.

Silence had worked once before. A different table, a different debt; she'd read it right that time, heard the apology folded inside the quiet like a note held instead of played. You were betting the same silence twice.

She moved a card. Moved another. Then two fingers hooked the rim of the bowl and dragged it across the jack of spades, and she ate — quick, unceremonious, a woman who'd decided somewhere around day two that hunger outranked pride and had stopped renegotiating.

"The sixty-woolong ones," she said, to the noodles. "Jet's stash. Look at you. Big spender."

Steam. Hull-hum. Somewhere below the deck, a pipe ticked as it cooled.

Here is the part you didn't get to see. What you meant, sitting there with your hands quiet and everything unspent: *I'm sorry.* Whole and rare and yours, offered the only way you offer anything — turned facedown, so it could be refused without anyone bleeding.

What she heard: nothing. Which she knew how to read. She'd been aboard long enough to learn this ship's dialect of nothing — nothing over a hospital bill meant *don't mention it*; nothing across a card table meant *fold and walk*. She turned your silence over once, checked it against the ledger she keeps where nobody is allowed to look, and filed it: *he doesn't think he owes you anything. Eat the noodles. They're just noodles.*

Wrong drawer. But it slid in smooth, and the drawer clicked shut.

She finished before you did. Stood, rinsed the bowl, set it upside down on the tower — seventh floor. Then she took the cigarette from behind her ear and didn't light it, just held it, like a woman checking her own pockets for something to feel.

"I've got a thing tomorrow," she said. "Off-ship. Solo." At the hatch she paused, not quite turning around. "Thanks for dinner. You'd have said, if there was something to say."

The hatch sighed shut on her heels.

Her game sat abandoned mid-cheat, red on red, going nowhere. The sentence was still there behind your teeth — six words, no lighter than before. You had until morning to spend it, and morning, on this ship, always came earlier than the money.
